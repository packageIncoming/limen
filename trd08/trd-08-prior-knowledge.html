TRD-08: Benchmark Harness

Prior Knowledge — Limen RDMA Transport Project
Depends on: TRD-07  |  Difficulty: Medium  |  Estimated: 3–4 sessions

1   Where This Fits

Eight rungs produced a transport with five tunable knobs and no idea what any of them do. TRD-07 was explicit about this: you built selectable mechanisms and were forbidden from recording performance claims, because you had measured nothing.

This rung measures. It is the last rung, and it converts a library into a result.

The engineering here is not RDMA — it is measurement, and measurement is a discipline with its own failure modes that are as subtle as the arming race and considerably more common. A benchmark that reports a mean latency is lying by omission. A benchmark that reports a number without a distribution is unfalsifiable. A benchmark that was never validated against a known-good implementation is measuring its own harness.

Two honest expectations before you start. First, most of TRD-07’s optimisations will show approximately nothing on this fabric, for the reason TRD-00 §9 gave and TRD-07 §1 repeated. Second, that null result is the most valuable thing this rung produces, because it names precisely what a hardware adapter contributes.

2   What Makes a Measurement Defensible

A number is defensible when someone who distrusts you can reproduce it or refute it. Five things are required:

PropertyWithout it
A stated methodologyNobody can tell what was measured, so nobody can disagree
A distribution, not a summaryThe tail — where the interesting behaviour lives — is invisible
Recorded conditionsNobody can reproduce it, including you next month
Run-to-run varianceA difference between configurations cannot be distinguished from noise
Validation against a referenceYou may be measuring your harness rather than your transport

The last is the one people skip. If your latency figure is four times a reference implementation’s on the same hardware, the most likely explanation is a defect in how you measure, not a fourfold difference in what you built.

3   Latency Is a Distribution

Report a mean latency and you have discarded the information that matters.

Latency distributions have fat tails: most samples cluster tightly and a few are ten to a thousand times larger.[4] A mean is dragged upward by those outliers while simultaneously concealing them — it is both unrepresentative of the typical case and uninformative about the bad one.

Report percentiles instead. The reference implementation does exactly this, and its column headers are worth reading carefully:

#bytes #iterations  t_min  t_max  t_typical  t_avg  t_stdev  99%  99.9%
     2        1000   1.49   3.93       1.50   1.51     0.07 1.57   3.93
t_typical is the median, not the mean

t_typical is the 50th percentile; t_avg is the arithmetic mean. They are printed side by side deliberately. Comparing them tells you how skewed the distribution is — within about ten percent of each other indicates a fairly flat distribution, and a larger gap indicates a tail worth investigating.[4]

Note the row above: median 1.50, mean 1.51, 99th 1.57, but 99.9th at 3.93 — more than double. That is a fat tail, and a report giving only the mean would have hidden it entirely while claiming the system was well-behaved.

Which percentiles to report: the median tells you the common case, the 99th tells you what a user notices, and the 99.9th tells you what happens under composition. A request touching ten services experiences one service’s 99.9th percentile roughly one percent of the time, so tail latency compounds in ways the median does not predict.

4   Coordinated Omission

This is the deepest methodological trap in latency measurement, it is nearly universal in hand-rolled benchmarks, and it is invisible in the code.

Consider the obvious loop:

for (i = 0; i < N; ++i) {
    t0 = now();
    send_and_wait_for_reply();
    record(now() - t0);
}

Suppose one operation stalls for 100 milliseconds. You record one sample of 100 ms. But during that stall, at a target rate of one operation every 2 microseconds, fifty thousand operations should have been issued and were not. Each of them would have experienced a queueing delay — the first would have waited nearly the full 100 ms, the next slightly less, and so on down to zero.

Your benchmark did not issue them, so it did not record them. The stall contributes exactly one bad sample out of a million instead of fifty thousand, and your 99.9th percentile reports as healthy while the system was unresponsive for a tenth of a second.

The name comes from the benchmark coordinating with the system under test: it politely stops issuing work precisely when the system is least able to handle it, which is the opposite of what a real client does.

This is not a small correction. On a system with occasional stalls, coordinated omission routinely understates high percentiles by an order of magnitude or more. A benchmark reporting a healthy 99.9th percentile while the system periodically freezes is the normal outcome of the loop above.

The two defences

Measure against a schedule, not against issue time. Decide the intended send time for operation i in advance — start + i × interval — and measure latency from that intended time rather than from when you actually managed to send. An operation delayed by a stall then correctly reports the delay plus the service time.

auto intended = t_start + i * interval;
/* if we are behind schedule, do NOT sleep — we are already late */
if (now() < intended) sleep_until(intended);
send_and_wait();
record(now() - intended);        /* from INTENDED, not from send */

Or report it as a throughput test and stop calling it latency. A closed-loop ping-pong with one operation outstanding measures round-trip service time under zero queueing. That is a legitimate and useful measurement — it is what the reference latency tools do — but it is not the latency a client would experience under load, and labelling it as such is the error.

This ladder’s harness does both, in separate modes, and labels them differently. The distinction between service time and response time is the whole content of this section.

5   Clocks

clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC) is the correct default. It never steps backwards, is unaffected by wall-clock adjustments, and on modern systems is implemented in the vDSO so a call costs tens of nanoseconds without entering the kernel.

CLOCK_REALTIME is wrong: it can jump when the system clock is adjusted, producing negative durations. CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW is unaffected by adjtime slewing but is slower and rarely worth it.

Reading the timestamp counter directly is tempting and is a trap unless you know the platform. It requires an invariant timestamp counter, needs serialisation to prevent the processor reordering the read across the code being measured, and produces cycles rather than nanoseconds so it needs calibration. On a virtual machine it may be trapped and emulated, which is far slower than the vDSO call it was supposed to replace.

Measure your clock before measuring with it

Time a loop of ten thousand consecutive clock_gettime calls with no work between them. That is your measurement floor. If it is 25 ns and you are measuring 1.5 µs operations, the overhead is under two percent and can be ignored. If you are measuring 100 ns operations, it cannot.

6   Warm-Up and Steady State

The first iterations of any benchmark are unrepresentative. Pages have not been faulted in, caches are cold, branch predictors are untrained, and on RDMA specifically the adapter’s own translation caches have not seen your addresses. The reference tools have a warm-up option for exactly this — iterations performed before measurement begins, to warm memory caches.[3]

Discard a fixed number of iterations before recording. A few hundred to a few thousand is typical, and the correct number is observable: plot latency against iteration number and find where it flattens.

The opposite failure is running so long that thermal throttling, memory fragmentation, or another process’s activity changes the machine underneath you. Reporting a single long run hides that; several shorter runs with reported variance does not.

7   Bandwidth

Bandwidth is measured with the opposite methodology to latency: keep many operations in flight and measure aggregate bytes over elapsed time.

Three things to get right:

Sustained, not peak. A peak figure over a short window is noise. The reference tools report both peak and average and the average is the meaningful one — TRD-00 R5 already had you record it for that reason.

Sweep the message size. A single figure at one size tells you almost nothing, because the shape of the curve is the result. Small messages are bounded by per-operation cost and large ones by the link, and where the curve turns is the interesting fact. Powers of two from 64 bytes to 1 MB is a standard sweep; the reference tools offer an all-sizes mode for this.[3]

State the units. MB/s and Gbit/s differ by a factor of eight and a decimal-versus-binary convention. The reference tools default to MiB/sec and offer a flag to report Gbit/sec instead.[3] Pick one, label every figure, and convert once.

Message rate — operations per second — is worth reporting separately for small sizes, where it is the limiting quantity rather than bytes.

8   Validation

Your harness is code you wrote and has bugs. The defence is to measure something whose answer is independently known.

Run ib_send_lat and ib_send_bw in a configuration matching yours as closely as you can, and compare. Agreement within a modest margin means your harness is probably sound. A large discrepancy means one of you is wrong, and the prior strongly favours the reference implementation.

Establish their convention before comparing

A latency test performs a round trip. Whether the tool reports the full round trip or half of it — the one-way estimate — is a convention you must establish rather than assume, because assuming wrongly produces a clean factor-of-two discrepancy that looks like a real difference.

Determine it empirically: measure a full round trip yourself with the same message size and iteration count, and see whether their figure matches yours or half of it. Record which. This is a five-minute experiment that prevents an entire report being wrong by a factor of two.

Match what you can: message size, iteration count, transport type, and whether warm-up is enabled. Differences that remain after matching are differences worth investigating.

9   What You Will Actually Measure Here

Honest expectations, so that the results are not mistaken for defects:

ComparisonExpected on soft-RoCEExpected on hardware
Inline vs notNo measurable differenceSubstantial at small sizes
Signal every 1 vs 16Little or noneMeaningful at high rates
Pipeline depth 1 vs 8Some improvementLarge improvement
Poll vs eventEvent measurably worseEvent measurably worse
One-sided vs two-sidedLittle differenceOne-sided clearly cheaper
Absolute latencyTens of microsecondsOne to three microseconds
Versus TCPTCP winsRDMA wins decisively

Two rows are worth dwelling on. Pipelining shows a real effect even here, because it addresses idle time rather than per-operation cost, and idle time exists on any fabric. Event-driven reaping is measurably worse on both, because wakeup cost is real regardless of what the transport is implemented in — it is the one row where the software provider gives you a true answer.

Everything else is flat because, as TRD-07 §1 said, these mechanisms reduce costs that are noise beside a transport running in software. Recording that honestly — with the numbers that show it — is a stronger conclusion than either “the optimisations work” or “they do not.”

11   What Is Deliberately Left Out

No statistical significance testing. Reporting variance across runs and refusing to conclude anything smaller than it is the right discipline at this scale; confidence intervals over a handful of runs of a deterministic system would be theatre.

No CPU-cycle accounting, no cache-miss counters, no flame graphs. Useful for finding why something is slow, and this rung establishes whether it is.

No multi-node or multi-connection scaling. Two nodes, one connection.

No comparison against other RDMA libraries. Validating against the reference micro-benchmarks is the check that matters; comparing against a full library would compare design decisions rather than mechanisms.

12   Further Reading

Read before starting:

  • Tene, “How NOT to Measure Latency”[1] — the canonical treatment of coordinated omission. An hour, and it will change how you read every benchmark you encounter afterwards.
  • ib_send_lat(1)[3] — the options, the warm-up flag, and the output columns you are validating against.

Read while implementing:

  • clock_gettime(2)[2] — the clock sources and their guarantees.
  • The perftest README[5] — its notes on testing methodology, which are brief and were written by people who had made the mistakes.

Reference as needed:

  • Lawrey, “What is Latency, Throughput and Degree of Concurrency?”[4] — the source of the median-versus-mean heuristic in §3 and a good short treatment of fat tails.
